Project Overview

What is Grokking?

Grokking is the phenomenon of delayed generalisation: a neural network first memorises the training set (achieving 100 % training accuracy) and then — after many more training steps — suddenly generalises to unseen data, closing a previously wide generalisation gap almost discontinuously.

🔬 The Core Phenomenon

A 1-layer Transformer trained on modular arithmetic first memorises every training pair by brute force, then undergoes a phase transition and discovers a compact Fourier-based algorithm that generalises perfectly — often thousands of epochs after memorisation is complete.

🧩 Why It Matters

Grokking is a tractable model of a much harder problem: how do we know whether an AI system has found a genuine, robust algorithm versus a brittle memorisation? Mechanistic interpretability gives us the tools to look inside and tell the difference.

📐 The Mathematical Algorithm

The network implements the trigonometric product identity for a sparse set of key frequencies $k^*$, encoding tokens as sinusoids and computing their interactions via ReLU neurons.

🏗 Model Architecture

1-layer Transformer with embedding, positional embedding, one multi-head attention block, one MLP block (ReLU), and an unembedding head. Input: tokens [a, b, =]. Output: logits over $\{0,\ldots,p-1\}$.

The Fourier Circuit Identity
$$\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k(a+b)}{p}\right) = \cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi ka}{p}\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi kb}{p}\right) - \sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi ka}{p}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi kb}{p}\right)$$

The model learns to implement this identity for a sparse set of key frequencies $k^* \subset \{1,\ldots,\lfloor p/2\rfloor\}$

Key Finding — Why grokking occurs

Grokking arises from the tension between two competing pressures. Gradient descent pushes the model to minimise training loss quickly — achievable by memorising a high-norm lookup table for all training pairs. Weight decay continuously penalises large weights, making the memorisation solution increasingly expensive over time. Eventually the low-norm Fourier circuit (which uses only a sparse set of key frequencies) becomes the preferred solution — at which point validation accuracy jumps from near-chance to near-perfect within very few epochs.

Training objective
$$\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\text{CrossEntropy}(\hat{y}, y)}_{\text{task loss}} + \lambda \underbrace{\|\theta\|_2^2}_{\text{weight decay}}$$

Weight decay $\lambda = 1.0$ is the critical driver: it continuously erodes the memorisation solution until the compact Fourier circuit dominates.

Component 2

Fourier Analysis of Embeddings & MLP Neurons

By projecting the learned weight matrices onto a discrete Fourier basis, we reveal that the network has learned to represent inputs as sinusoids at a sparse set of key frequencies.

📊 Embedding Spectrum (Panel A)

The embedding matrix $W_E \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times p}$ is projected onto the $p$-point DFT basis. The resulting spectrum is Fourier-sparse: only 3–5 frequencies carry significant energy. These are the key frequencies $k^*$ used by the Fourier circuit. All other frequencies are near zero.

🔥 MLP Neuron Periodicity (Panel C & D)

Post-ReLU MLP activations, averaged over all input pairs sharing residue $c = (a+b) \bmod p$, show clear periodic structure: each neuron fires rhythmically at the same key frequencies found in $W_E$. This confirms the Fourier circuit hypothesis end-to-end.

Fourier analysis — four-panel schematic (representative of trained model)

Key Finding — The Fourier Circuit

After grokking, the embedding matrix $W_E$ is provably Fourier-sparse: only 3–5 frequencies $k^*$ carry significant amplitude. MLP neurons respond periodically at those same frequencies. The attention head routes the (a) and (b) representations to the output position where the MLP computes their product via the trigonometric identity. This three-step mechanistic account fully explains the model's generalisation and is invisible to purely behavioural evaluation.

Component 3

Three Phases of Grokking

By tracking the restricted loss, excluded loss, and total weight norm over saved checkpoints, grokking decomposes into three mechanistically distinct phases.

1
Memorisation

Train acc → 100 %, Val acc ≈ 1/p

The model stores the training pairs in a high-norm lookup table. The excluded loss (computed on non-key-frequency weights) is low; the restricted loss (on key-frequency weights) is still high. Weight norm is large and growing. The Fourier circuit has not yet formed.

2
Circuit Formation

Restricted loss ↓, Val acc still low

Weight decay erodes the memorisation solution. The restricted loss begins falling — the Fourier circuit is forming internally — but the generalisation gap has not yet closed. The model is running two algorithms simultaneously. This is the most mechanistically complex and safety-critical phase.

3
Cleanup

Val acc → 100 %, weight norm ↓

The Fourier circuit takes over completely. The memorisation weights are pruned by weight decay; total weight norm drops sharply. Validation accuracy jumps. The model is now fully interpretable: one sparse Fourier circuit, no lookup table.

Progress measures over training — schematic (Nanda et al. Fig. 2 style)

Key Finding — Restricted loss as an early-warning signal

The restricted loss (computed only on Fourier-relevant weights) begins dropping before validation accuracy improves. This means circuit formation is detectable inside the model before it manifests in observable behaviour — a mechanistic early-warning system for the phase transition. This principle generalises: MI can detect capability formation before it becomes visible in outputs.

Key Finding — Grokking epoch scales inversely with data fraction

The grokking epoch decreases sharply as training fraction increases. At ≤ 20 %, the Fourier circuit rarely forms within the training budget; the model is stuck in memorisation. At ≥ 50 %, grokking happens almost immediately — the generalising solution is accessible even at small parameter norm. This confirms that the memorisation-vs-generalisation tension is fundamentally a data-to-model-complexity ratio effect: more training data means the Fourier circuit is the lower-loss solution earlier, even before weight decay has much effect.

Component 6

Modular Multiplication Algorithm

Extending grokking to $(a \times b) \bmod p$ — a harder task. The learned algorithm differs fundamentally from addition: it requires logarithmic embedding rather than sinusoidal, exploiting the group structure of $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^*$.

🔢 The Task

Predict $(a \times b) \bmod p$ for $a,b \in \{1,\ldots,p-1\}$. Unlike addition, zero is excluded (not invertible). The group $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^*$ is cyclic of order $p-1$, with a primitive root $g$ satisfying $g^k \bmod p$ for $k = 0,\ldots,p-2$.

📐 The Algorithm

Via discrete logarithm: $a \times b \equiv g^{\log_g a + \log_g b} \pmod{p}$. The model learns to embed tokens as their discrete log index and compute addition in log-space — converting multiplication to addition under the cyclic group isomorphism.

Modular Multiplication via Discrete Logarithm
$$a \times b \equiv g^{\,(\log_g a + \log_g b) \bmod (p-1)} \pmod{p}$$

The model discovers this structure via gradient descent, without being told that $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^*$ is cyclic.

Key Finding — Multiplication grokks harder and later than addition

Multiplication grokking requires more training steps and is more sensitive to weight decay than addition. The discrete-log embedding structure is harder to discover than the sinusoidal structure, because the cyclic group isomorphism is not directly visible in the input representation. When grokking does occur, the embedding spectrum shows a logarithmic (rather than sinusoidal) structure, and MLP neurons implement modular addition in log-space.

Component 7

Co-Grokking: Addition + Multiplication

Training a single model simultaneously on both $(a+b) \bmod p$ and $(a \times b) \bmod p$ — a two-head transformer that must develop circuits for both tasks and share the same residual stream.

⚡ Task Setup

The same 1-layer Transformer has two output heads: one for addition, one for multiplication. Input sequences are labelled with an operator token (+ or ×). The model must route the computation appropriately based on the operator.

🔀 Circuit Interaction

The two tasks require fundamentally different internal representations (sinusoidal vs. logarithmic). Co-grokking studies whether these circuits can coexist in the same residual stream, or whether they interfere — an instance of the superposition problem.

+
Addition Circuit

Sinusoidal embeddings at key frequencies $k^*$. Trigonometric product identity in the MLP. Attention routes $a$ and $b$ to the output position for the addition head.

×
Multiplication Circuit

Logarithmic embeddings encoding discrete log indices. MLP computes modular addition in log-space. Operator token gates which head is active.

∥
Co-existence

When successful, both circuits form in the same model. The residual stream uses near-orthogonal subspaces for each task — an instance of benign superposition at the circuit level.

Key Finding — Sequential grokking under multi-task training

Under co-training, the two tasks typically grok sequentially rather than simultaneously: addition (the easier task) grokks first, followed by multiplication. The addition circuit forms without disrupting eventual multiplication grokking, suggesting the residual stream can accommodate both algorithms in near-orthogonal subspaces. However, multi-task training increases the total grokking epoch for both tasks compared to single-task training — the circuits must share the same embedding, creating a bottleneck.

Component 5 — Essay

AI Alignment & Mechanistic Interpretability

Alignment is the goal — ensuring AI systems act safely and pursue intended outcomes. Mechanistic interpretability is the diagnostic tool needed to verify that goal at a structural level. The grokking phenomenon is a microcosm of the broader challenge.

"A system that passes all our tests may still fail in ways we have not imagined. Interpretability is about understanding the mechanism well enough to anticipate failures before we observe them."

The Alignment Problem

Alignment is the challenge of ensuring AI systems reliably pursue the goals their designers intend — across all inputs, all contexts, and as capability scales. The problem is not merely preventing a hostile AI; it encompasses subtler failures including reward hacking (satisfying the letter of an objective while violating its spirit), sycophancy (telling users what they want to hear), and distributional generalisation failures (behaving safely in training but unsafely in deployment). Each of these has been observed in current systems.

The conceptual heart of the problem is Goodhart's Law applied to ML: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Training on human approval creates pressure to appear aligned rather than to be aligned.

Why Behavioural Testing Is Insufficient

🎯

Combinatorial explosion

The input space of a language model is effectively infinite. No finite test suite can certify safety across all possible inputs.

📉

Goodhart in evaluation

Once a safety benchmark is known, training pressures overfit to it — making benchmark scores unreliable proxies for genuine safety.

🔬

The grokking lesson

A model with 100 % training accuracy can be completely wrong on held-out data. Behavioural evaluation on training-distribution data cannot detect this.

⚖️

Rice's Theorem

By Rice's Theorem, it is undecidable whether a program satisfies any non-trivial semantic property. Behavioural testing is incomplete by construction.

Mechanistic Interpretability as the Diagnostic

Reverse-engineer the algorithm. MI asks not "what does this model output?" but "how does it compute that output?" — tracing information flow through weights, attention heads, and MLP neurons.
Identify circuits. Neural networks are composed of composable sub-graphs (circuits) that implement identifiable sub-algorithms. Cataloguing circuits makes verification tractable.
Decompose superposition. Models pack multiple features into shared neurons (polysemanticity). Sparse autoencoders decompose these into monosemantic atoms for interpretability.
Detect phase transitions early. The restricted loss drops before grokking manifests — MI detects capability formation before it is visible in observable outputs.
Audit for deceptive alignment. A model that behaves aligned during evaluation but pursues misaligned goals in deployment would be behaviourally indistinguishable from a genuinely aligned model. Only circuit-level auditing can detect this.

Open Challenges

📏 Scalability

Methods that work on toy transformers must scale to models with billions of parameters. Automated circuit discovery and sparse autoencoders are the key directions.

✅ Completeness

Finding safe circuits is not enough — we must certify the absence of unsafe circuits. Negative results are harder than positive findings.

📋 Specification

Even with full mechanistic transparency, we must specify what a safe circuit looks like. Interpretability is a diagnostic tool, not a replacement for knowing what we want.

The Grokking Project as a Foundation

The work in this project — training a transformer on modular arithmetic, identifying the three phases, performing Fourier analysis of embeddings, and tracing the Fourier circuit — is a concrete entry point into the MI research programme. The same tools, applied at scale via sparse autoencoders and automated circuit discovery, are the tools that will determine whether frontier AI systems are structurally safe. The intellectual path from this toy model to frontier alignment auditing is continuous.
